Retirement Planning at 50: An Honest Assessment

At 50, you have less time for compounding but several distinct advantages:

  • Peak earnings — most professionals are at or near their highest salary between 45 and 55, providing maximum capacity to save.
  • Catch-up contributions — the IRS allows an additional $7,500/year in 401(k) contributions once you turn 50 (on top of the standard $23,000 limit for 2024). That is $30,500 total, or about $2,540/month of pre-tax savings.
  • Fewer financial obligations — if your mortgage is partially paid off and children are nearing independence, you may have more disposable income than at any other point in your life.
  • Flexibility on retirement age — working until 67 or 70 instead of 65 adds 2–5 more years of contributions and growth, which can increase your balance by 30–50%.

The default retirement age above is set to 67 — the full Social Security retirement age for most people in this age group — to give you a more realistic 17-year window.

Catch-Up Strategies for 50-Year-Olds

  • Max out tax-advantaged accounts — contribute the full $30,500 (including catch-up) to your 401(k). If you have a Roth IRA, add another $8,000. These pre-tax and tax-free vehicles amplify your savings.
  • Consider delaying retirement — each additional year you work means one more year of contributions, one more year of growth, and one fewer year of withdrawals. Working to 67 instead of 65 can increase your nest egg by 20%+.
  • Delay Social Security — waiting until 70 to claim increases your monthly benefit by about 8% per year beyond your full retirement age. From 67 to 70, that is a 24% permanent increase in monthly income.
  • Reduce expenses now — consider downsizing, paying off remaining debts, and cutting discretionary spending to maximize the amount you can funnel into retirement accounts.
  • Use a slightly conservative allocation — with 15–17 years, a 60/40 or 70/30 stock/bond split is common. You still need growth, but you have less time to recover from a major downturn.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much should I have saved by 50?

Fidelity recommends having 6x your annual salary saved by 50. On a $90,000 salary, that is $540,000. If you are below this benchmark, focus on maximizing contributions and catch-up limits. Remember that guidelines are targets, not requirements — any savings you have is a foundation to build on.

Can I retire comfortably if I start saving at 50?

It depends on your savings rate, current savings, expected Social Security benefits, and desired lifestyle. With aggressive saving ($1,500–$2,500/month), some existing savings, and Social Security, many people who start at 50 can build enough for a comfortable — if not lavish — retirement. Consider working with a financial advisor to create a detailed plan.

Should I use a lower expected return at this age?

Yes, we default to 6% here instead of 7% because a 50-year-old's portfolio should typically be somewhat more conservative than a 30-year-old's. With less time to recover from downturns, a moderate allocation (60–70% stocks, 30–40% bonds) is common, which historically returns closer to 6% after inflation.
